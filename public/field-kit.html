Lookalikes that trip people up

Powderpost beetles

Round exit holes 1/32–1/8 in. with fine, flour-like powder. Mostly hardwoods and older softwood framing.

Old vs. active damage

Dry, brittle mud tubes and broken-open galleries with no workers inside can be old. Break a tube: if it is rebuilt in a few days, it is active.

Damage plus decay

Termites and carpenter ants move into wood that is already wet. Expect to find both and report both.

Mud dauber and ant debris

Mud dauber nests are blob- or pipe-organ-shaped and not attached to soil. Ant nests along slabs can push out soil that looks like termite dirt.

Before and during the walk-through

Questions to ask

Ask the homeowner or agent before you start. Their answers tell you where to look first and what to write up.

History

  1. Has the house ever been treated for termites or other wood-destroying insects?

    Look for treatment stickers on the water heater or electrical panel and ask for paperwork.

  2. Is there an active termite bond or warranty? With which company, and is it transferable?
  3. Has any damage been repaired? When, and was it repaired before or after treatment?

    Fresh paint, new sill sections or sistered joists deserve a closer look.

  4. What year was the house built? Any additions, porches or sunrooms added later?

    Additions often sit on a separate slab with an untreated joint.

What they have seen

  1. Have you seen winged insects indoors? What month, what time of day, and after rain?

    Spring daytime swarms after rain point to subterranean termites. Evening swarms and lights point to drywood termites or ants.

  2. Have you found piles of wings on windowsills or in light fixtures?
  3. Have you seen large black or red-and-black ants inside, especially at night?
  4. Have you noticed sawdust, small pellets or dirt appearing in the same spot?
  5. Any floors that feel soft, doors or windows that stick, or paint that bubbles?

Moisture

  1. Any roof, plumbing or appliance leaks, past or present?
  2. Does the crawlspace or basement ever flood or stay damp?
  3. Is there a sump pump, dehumidifier or crawlspace vapor barrier? Is it working?
  4. Where do the gutters and downspouts drain? Is there irrigation near the foundation?

Access and conditions

  1. Are there any areas I can't get to: finished basement, stored items, locked rooms, sealed crawlspace?
  2. Is there rigid foam insulation, stucco or siding that runs below grade?
  3. Any firewood, lumber or debris stored against the house or in the crawlspace?
  4. Any wood that touches soil: deck posts, fence attachments, porch steps, trellises?
Stop, flag or limit

Knockout factors

Findings that decide the report, block a clean letter or warranty, or limit what you can state. Adjust this list to match your company's policy and your state's rules.

Stop decides the report or blocks a clean letter Flag needs further evaluation or treatment first Limit must be disclosed as a limitation
Stop

Live termites or active infestation

Live workers, soldiers or swarmers, or tubes that are rebuilt after you break them. Report as active evidence and recommend treatment. No clean letter.

Stop

Visible evidence without a treatment record

Tubes, damage or frass with no proof of a prior treatment. Report the evidence and recommend treatment even if it looks old.

Stop

Structural damage to load-bearing members

Sills, joists, beams or posts that are hollowed, crushed or sagging. Recommend evaluation by a qualified contractor or engineer. Do not estimate strength yourself.

Flag

Rigid foam or EIFS below grade

Termites tunnel through foam out of sight. Many companies will not warranty the structure until it is cut back and an inspection gap is left.

Flag

Wood-to-soil contact

Framing, posts, steps or siding touching soil. Record it as a conducive condition and recommend correction.

Flag

Excess moisture in framing

Readings above about 20% in structural wood, standing water, or a failed vapor barrier. Find the source before calling it insect damage alone.

Flag

Earth-filled porches and slab additions

Filled porches, stoops and additions give hidden entry into the house. Often need drilling and treatment before any warranty.

Limit

Inaccessible or obstructed areas

Crawlspace with less than 18 in. clearance, finished basement walls, stored items, floor coverings or locked rooms. List each area on the report.

Limit

Weather or site conditions

Snow cover, standing water in the crawl, or unsafe conditions such as live wiring, animals or sewage. Note them and offer a re-inspection.

For inspectors

FAQ

How do I tell old termite damage from active infestation?

Look for live insects first. Break a small section of mud tube and look for workers, then re-check in a few days to see if it has been repaired. Moist, dark soil in galleries suggests activity; dry, crumbly soil and empty galleries suggest old damage. If you can't confirm either way, report the evidence as present and say that activity was not observed.

Can a photo alone confirm termites?

No. A photo can show that damage matches termites, ants or decay, but the call comes from what you see, probe and measure on site. Use the photo check as a second opinion and a record for the file.

Do I report carpenter ants on a WDI report?

Yes. Carpenter ants are wood-destroying insects on the standard NPMA-33 form, along with termites, wood-boring beetles and carpenter bees. Report visible evidence and damage. Fungus and decay are usually reported as conducive conditions, not as insect findings.

What moisture readings should I worry about?

Framing in a healthy house usually reads 8–15%. Above about 20% is conducive to decay and insects. Near 28–30% (fiber saturation) decay fungi grow actively. Make sure your meter is set for the wood species and adjust for temperature.

A swarmer was found indoors but I see no other evidence. What do I write?

A swarmer indoors is evidence of a colony nearby, often inside the structure. Identify it (termite or ant), report it as evidence, note where it was found, and recommend a full evaluation. Keep the specimen in a vial if you can.

How do I tell drywood termite pellets from carpenter ant frass?

Drywood pellets are hard, uniform, six-sided grains about 1 mm long that roll like sand. Carpenter ant frass is soft, fibrous and uneven, like sawdust, and often has insect legs or heads in it. A hand lens settles it in seconds.

Should I probe finished surfaces?

Sound and probe exposed wood freely. On finished surfaces, tap and listen for hollow spots, and use a moisture meter. Do not damage finishes without the owner's permission; list the area as a limitation instead.

What should my photos include?

One wide shot that shows where the damage is, one close-up with a scale reference (coin, ruler or tape), and one shot of any frass, tubes or insects. Good light matters more than resolution: use a flashlight held at a low angle to show texture.
